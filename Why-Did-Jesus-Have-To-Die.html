Why Did Jesus Have to Die?

A Conversation with a Muslim Student

By Kranthi Erra • Nov 18, 2026 • Faith & Apologetics

Last night, I had a long conversation with a Muslim student about faith as I was tabling for BASIC at Oswego. When I asked why he wasn’t a Christian, his answer was simple and honest: “I don’t think Jesus needed to die for God to forgive us.”

It is one of the most common questions in Christian-Muslim dialogue. If God is all-powerful and merciful, why couldn’t He just accept our repentance and let us into heaven?

Forgiveness Is Never Free

To answer that, I started with something we all understand: human forgiveness.

Let’s say someone betrayed you, slandered you, or destroyed something you value. Forgiving them is never free. You might tell them, “I forgive you,” without making them pay you back, but the debt doesn’t magically disappear. You pay it. It costs you your pride, your self-respect, your right to get even, and the quiet pain of absorbing the hurt yourself. Forgiveness always costs the one who forgives.

What Does Forgiveness Cost God?

The bigger question is, if forgiving a friend costs us our pride, what does it cost God to forgive a sinner?

Of course, God doesn’t have a fragile ego or pride to get over like men do. What is at stake for God is something far greater: His holiness and justice. If a human judge looked at a guilty criminal who said “I’m sorry” and let them walk free with zero penalty, we wouldn’t call that judge merciful; we’d call them a corrupt judge. In the same way, a perfectly righteous God cannot take the broom and sweep sin under the rug without ceasing to be good.

As theologian Augustus Strong noted, the atonement is fundamentally a necessity internal to God Himself: His justice must be satisfied so He can remain righteous while pardoning the sinner. That is why the Bible says God put Christ forward as a sacrifice so that He might be both “just and the justifier” (Romans 3:25–26), and why “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (Hebrews 9:22). Sin carries a real moral debt.

The Instinct for a Substitute

During our conversation, I brought up an intense topic within Islamic tradition itself. The Quran repeatedly refers to Allah as the Most Merciful. Yet in Sahih Muslim 2767a–b, the companion Abu Musa reports that the Prophet said that on the Day of Resurrection, Allah will deliver a Jew or a Christian to every Muslim as their “rescue from Hell-Fire,” placing them in the fire in the Muslim’s stead.

That raises the exact same question my friend asked me: if Allah is merciful, why can’t He just forgive without a substitute? Even if Islamic scholars interpret that passage differently than literal sin-transfer, it reveals an instinct buried in the human conscience: justice demands a substitute.

The question isn’t whether forgiveness has a cost, but who pays it.

The God Who Pays the Debt Himself

If Jesus’s death weren’t necessary for sin, then as 19th-century scholar Enoch Pond argued, the crucifixion of a perfectly holy man under God’s providence would be impossible to justify. But the gospel tells a different story. Moved by infinite compassion, God didn’t offload the penalty onto some random, unwilling bystander. In Jesus, God stepped into our world and absorbed the full weight of His own justice Himself.

Jesus clearly said He wasn’t a victim, and He wasn’t reluctant. Speaking of His own death, He declared, “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord” (John 10:18). He came willingly to take our place, fulfilling Isaiah’s prophecy: “the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all” (Isaiah 53:6).

Forgiveness costs us our pride. Forgiveness cost God His Son. Jesus didn’t die because God was reluctant to forgive; He died because God loved us too much to leave us in our sin (John 3:16) and was too just to pretend it didn’t matter.
